RABBITSTEW · PROGRESS REPORT 2 · 4 OCTOBER 2026 · AS OF 3 OCTOBER, 13:00 UTC

Back to the original question

Rabbitstew began as a 2005 proposal to test whether robots that evolve their bodies and brains together beat robots that evolve only a brain inside a fixed, designed body. The first answer was no. This report explains where that question came from, how the simulator's genome, brain and evolution work, and what the first benchmark of its evolvability found. Both kinds of robot respond to selection. The benchmark's most flattering reading, that the evolving bodies respond more, turned out to rest on a loophole in the body model. Finding it before it could decide a larger experiment is part of what a benchmark is for. Since then, an audit has turned that lesson into rules for a fair experiment, the fixes are in, and the first stage of a sweep across 36 worlds has finished. Its provisional reading is that which body earns more depends on the world. The evidence on the evolving bodies’ side is thin, and a bug found in the physics engine is being checked before the next stage.

Where this comes from

The proposal. In December 2005, Ethan G. Jucovy proposed Rabbitstew: A Robot Simulator with Variable Morphologies as a senior conference project at Swarthmore College (the proposal, PDF). It asked one question. Does holistic evolution, where a robot's body and its brain evolve together from random shapes, produce better robots than conventional evolution, where only the brain evolves inside a fixed body a person designed? It noted that no one had run the two side by side. It proposed building a simulator that could grow and mutate bodies, then racing the two populations to the centre of an arena, after Karl Sims’s evolved virtual creatures.

Its prediction. Holistic evolution would lose early, because the designed body starts with a head start, and win eventually. It gave three reasons:

  1. Body-specific strategies. Evolving bodies find simple strategies that hinge on the quirks of their own bodies.
  2. Variability. A more varied population puts stronger selection on general, adaptable strategies.
  3. The extradimensional bypass (Michael Conrad, 1990). Adding dimensions to a search, here the shape of the body, can open ridges between fitness peaks that are cut off from each other when only the brain can change.

The proposal says openly that its author’s sympathies lay with holistic evolution. That is one reason every test here is registered before it runs: so that “no” can stand.

The first answer. The simulator was built twenty years later, and the experiment was run as written (Rabbitstew, Twenty Years On: Does Co-evolving the Body Beat a Designed One?). Each apparent holistic win turned out to be an artefact that an evolving body could find and a wheeled box could not. First, bodies grew to several times the fixed body’s mass, which wins a shoving contest regardless of control. Second, our spawn protocol dropped bodies from a height, and some turned the drop into momentum and rolled onto a raised plateau without a working motor. With both corrected, the fixed body’s controller also free to grow, and terrain drawn at random, the fixed body won on every seed: 2,088 holistic wins in 10,200 champion bouts. The first reason came true, but in a perverse form: the body-specific strategies exploited the simulator, not the task.

Why the programme moved to an ecology. A single competitive score between two robots turned out to be a poor instrument, as the proposal itself warned. The ecology follows Branen Salmon’s 2003 argument, from which the proposal takes the term “holistic evolution”: pair it with embodied evolution, in which selection and reproduction happen between robots in the world they live in, with no central judge (Watson, Ficici and Pollack, 1999). In the ecology, robots eat, breed and die, and nobody scores them. It let the programme first ask a more basic question, whether evolution works in this simulator at all, and it does for movement and foraging (report 1). The plan was always to return to the original question once evolution and the world could both be measured. The benchmark in this report is the first such measurement.

Terms, defined

Holistic body, fixed body
A holistic robot evolves its body and brain together. The fixed body is a hand-designed wheeled robot, loosely modelled on a Pioneer research robot, whose brain alone evolves.
Fauna
One of these two populations: the evolving bodies or the fixed body.
Seed
The number that fixes every random draw in a run. Runs on different seeds are independent repeats of the same design.
Planted
Written into the founders’ genomes by us at the start, rather than evolved.
Held
Still carried in working form, after a set number of seasons, by more descendants than mutation alone would leave.
Lesion
Cutting a circuit’s connections in an evolved robot and measuring what it loses. If the gain disappears, the circuit was doing the work.
Null
What a test should see if the effect it looks for is absent, for example the same run with selection switched off. Results are read against it.
Power
The chance that a test would detect an effect of a stated size if it were real. Every “nothing found” states it.
Selection line: up, down, control
A lineage bred for a set number of generations from parents we choose: the highest scorers (up), the lowest (down) or a random draw (control).
Realised heritability
How much of the selection applied shows up in the offspring: the response per generation divided by how far the chosen parents stood from the average. 0 means nothing is passed on; 1 means all of it is.

How the machinery works

The design follows the 2005 proposal, which in turn follows Karl Sims (1994). The physics engine is MuJoCo. Three parts matter for the results: how a genome becomes a body, how the brain runs, and how evolution proceeds without a fitness function. File names below point to the code.

The genome and the body

  • A genome is a directed graph (genotype.py). Each node describes one kind of body part: a box, sphere or cylinder, with its proportions and its own small brain. Each connection says where a child part attaches on its parent’s surface, at what angle and size, and with which joint: hinge, ball, slider or fixed.
  • Recursion makes repeated parts. A connection may point back to its own node, or to an ancestor. Its recursive limit caps how many copies of that node can follow in a row, so one node can build a whole limb of segments.
  • Synthesis is breadth-first (synthesis.py). The body is built outward from the root, one layer at a time, until a part limit is reached: twice the number of nodes, and never more than 64. Building layer by layer expresses as many different nodes as possible before the limit bites. Nodes never reached are carried silently, like recessive genes.
  • Size and mass limits. Every part is between 0.06 and 0.6 m across, a child is scaled from its parent, and every part has the same density. An optional mass budget scales down the mass of any robot heavier than the fixed body’s 15 kg. That is the correction from the first answer.

A two-node genome and the body it builds. Parts are numbered in the order they are made: the root, then everything one joint away, then two joints away. Below part 2, a third B in a row would break B’s limit of 2. Below part 3, a second B would be allowed, but a two-node genome may build only four parts, so it is left out. Each part carries its own copy of its node’s brain.

The brain

  • Local and global brains (genotype.py). Every body part carries its own local brain. A robot may also have one global brain, made only of neurons, that can connect to any local unit. So a controller can be spread across the body, centralised, or both.
  • Three kinds of unit. Sensors read the world: touch, which way is up, the part’s own velocity, joint angle and speed, height, a free-running oscillator, the smell of food and the nearness of other robots. Neurons only process. Effectors drive the motor on the joint that attaches their part to its parent.
  • The units are mostly tanh units. A unit adds up its weighted inputs and its own resting bias, and squashes the total into the range −1 to 1. Neurons may also use a few other shapes, such as sin or a leaky integrator. Effectors always use tanh.
  • The per-tick update (brain.py). At synthesis, all of a robot’s brains are folded into one network. Every tick (every 4 physics steps, 50 times a simulated second at the default timestep), sensors are overwritten with fresh readings and every other unit updates at once from the previous tick’s values. Effectors that drive the same joint axis are summed and clipped to ±1, and that sets the motor’s torque.

new activity of a unit = tanh( its bias + Σ weight × each input’s activity at the last tick )

One sensor, neuron and effector per part is the simplest case; evolution can add or remove units and links in any brain. Solid arrows are links inside a local brain, and dashed ones are links from the global brain. Every link has its own weight. Each tick, every unit reads what its inputs were a tick earlier.

Evolution

  • The ecology replaces a fitness function (ecology.py). Each season, each robot gets energy for what it eats, less the cost of its motors’ work where the world charges for it, and pays a fixed living cost. A robot with no energy starves, and one that reaches 60 seasons dies of age. A robot that banks 3 units of energy breeds if a slot is free, passing 1 unit to its child. Nobody is ranked or culled: what breeds shapes the next generation.
  • Crossover (genetics.py). About 30% of births mix in a second parent of the same kind. For evolving bodies it follows Sims: the child takes the first few nodes of one parent’s genome and the rest from the other. For the fixed body the body cannot change, so the parents’ controllers are mixed: weight by weight, or one parent’s global brain with the other’s local brains.
  • Mutation of the body, evolving bodies only: a part’s shape and proportions; where and at what angle a connection attaches, and its scale; the joint type, axis and limits; recursive limits; and adding or removing nodes and connections.
  • Mutation of the brain, both kinds: each weight and bias is nudged with probability 0.25 (and occasionally redrawn from scratch); units and links are added and removed; a neuron’s function is occasionally redrawn.
  • The global-bias walk. The nudges to the resting biases of the global brain’s neurons add up to a random walk. Report 1 found that this walk does most of the damage to a planted compass in the fixed body. The frozen-bias setting sets that step to zero. It applies to the fixed body only.

There is no score and no judge. The only link between what a robot does and how many copies of its genome survive is energy: eating more means breeding sooner and starving less. Report 1’s How time works sets out the season and the run.

New since report 1

How evolvable is the simulator?

Report 1 promised a single number for how strongly this simulator responds to selection, to measure any later change against. This is that benchmark (the study How evolvable is the simulator?), together with a comparison registered before any data were read (Does the more variable population respond more strongly to selection?). Both were run blind and passed independent review with binding caveats, which are given below in full.

The design. Here we choose the parents; this is not the ecology. From random founders, each generation of 40 robots is scored alone on net yield: food eaten minus the energy spent on motor work, in a 15-second solo run. The top 10 parent the up line, the bottom 10 the down line and a random 10 the control line. That ran for 24 generations on 12 seeds, for both faunas, under default mutation and under the frozen-bias setting.

The registered comparison asked whether the evolving bodies respond more strongly to selection than the fixed body, as the proposal’s second reason predicts.   • Capped, it is a tie. With the evolving down line’s work limited to what the fixed body’s down line can do, the difference is −0.02.
  • It says nothing for the second reason. That reason needs the evolving bodies to be the more varied population. On this trait they were the less varied, with a founder spread of 0.22 against 0.75, so a win for them cannot run through that mechanism.
  • The registered prediction was wrong, and why. Before the data we predicted the fixed body would respond more. The model behind that had no floors or ceilings: in fact, the fixed body’s down line hit the fixed limit of its own motors, while the evolving bodies escaped theirs by growing motors. Future registrations will state each body’s floor and ceiling in advance.

The frozen-bias setting makes no change

For the fixed body, freezing the global-bias walk changed the response to selection by −0.001 of a founder standard deviation per generation [−0.012, +0.009], inside the ±0.05 band registered in advance as “no change”. Report 1 found that freezing it helps keep a planted compass; this says it neither raises nor lowers the fixed body’s general response to selection. The setting does not apply to the evolving bodies, so there is no before-and-after number for them.

Why a benchmark whose best reading dissolved is still worth having

  • It gives the number it promised. Both faunas now have a measured response, a heritability index and a reference scale. Any change to the operator or the world can be measured against them.
  • It found a loophole before the loophole decided anything bigger. Every coming comparison between the two kinds of body is scored in net yield, and every down line in raw yield would have found this lever. Motor capacity will be budgeted before those comparisons run.
  • It showed the review working. The registered verdict favoured the evolving bodies, against our own prediction. Review traced it to an allowance of the body model. The headline did not survive, and it was stopped before it was published.
  • It said what the evolving bodies’ food gain is: movement and coverage, not perception. That tells us what the world is rewarding, which is the next section’s subject.

The food-compass results come from the fixed body only

Every food-compass result so far, including report 1’s Does a compass evolve where it pays more? and Is mutation the stall?, used the fixed-body robot and only that robot. No evolving body has carried a compass in any test. Report 1 said so in passing; it matters enough to say plainly.

Why the fixed body. It is the controllable lab animal. Its body never changes, so a compass can be defined on its two wheels, planted, lesioned and counted, and any change in foraging can be traced to the brain. The compass as built cannot be planted in an evolving body, whose sensors and joints are different every time.

It is also the control arm for the bypass test. The compass is a textbook fitness valley: a lone sensor makes the robot spin and costs it food, and the parts pay only once they arrive together with the right signs. On the fixed body we have measured that valley: mutation proposes the right shape in 84 of 200,000 lineages. The proposal’s third reason predicts that evolving bodies find a way around valleys like this one more readily. Testing that needs a body-general definition of the compass and the fixed body’s numbers to compare against.

Others found these first. Evolution exploiting its simulator is well documented (Lehman and colleagues, 2020, collect many cases), and Sims warned in 1994 that any energy leak “will inevitably be discovered and exploited by the evolving creatures”. Sims settled bodies before scoring; ours matches his, though it was written without reference to it. Taylor and Massey (2001) published stability checks and the handling of numerical explosions. Sims also scaled motor strength with the cross-section of the parts a joint connects, and clamped it. Our motor budget caps the whole body’s total, which goes beyond Sims’s per-motor cap; the area-scaling idea is his.

The design pass (the study Where can evolution buy fitness without the behaviour we mean?) hunts for the rest before they decide an experiment. Three independent auditors each read one layer of the simulator, without changing it:

  • Physics and the body model: motors, damping, joint limits, contacts, the mass budget, spawning, size limits, the timestep and sensors.
  • The genetic operators: what mutation and crossover make easy or hard to reach, including the bias walk and recursive limits.
  • The ecology and its economy: food, work and living costs, breeding, regrowth, crowding, and whether the world makes perception pay more than coverage.

Each auditor ranked what can be bought and how, whether we had already seen it exploited, which results it could affect, and a fix that is off by default and shown to close it. A fourth review read the project’s own history. An independent check then corrected some of the audits’ headlines, and the findings were combined into one set of rules a fair experiment in this simulator must meet. Everything below uses the corrected forms.

What the audit found

In one sentence: at present the simulator pays more readily for blind motion than for perception, and it lets motor capacity that costs nothing be spent on limbs spinning freely.

  • Motor strength multiplies with limb count. Each driven joint axis gets its own motor, sized to the heavier of the two parts it joins, and a ball joint carries three. A hub with twelve children reaches about seventeen times the fixed body’s motor strength per kilogram.
  • Limbs spinning freely. 97% of the evolving-body down line’s wasted work is done by limbs touching nothing, and 94% is on ball joints, which have no range. About a third is on limbs buried inside their parent. Parts joined rigidly are treated as one piece for collisions, so a limb can pass through them.
  • Bodies still settling when the clock starts. With motors off, 14 of 120 evolving bodies drift more than a quarter of a metre; none of 120 fixed bodies do. Part of that drift is rolling on uneven ground.
  • Coverage pays. A blind body spinning one motor at full throttle nets about +0.7 net-yield units a season, whatever the length of its limbs. Its gain comes from travelling, not reach.
  • A flat road to perception. In a simplified model of a forager under the world’s food and smell rules (a model, not the simulator), 25% more speed pays 13–40% more food, against 0–10% for a one-step better nose, in all 18 combinations of world settings tried.
  • A weak reward for foraging well. In the one evolved lineage measured, 60% of births starve. The economy selects hard on staying alive, and weakly on anything above that. How weakly depends on each world’s net income relative to the cost of living.

The history. The fourth review counts 73 claims walked back across the record: 23 in instruments, 14 statistical, 12 in the economy, 11 in the physics, 7 in wording and 6 in the operators. None was caught by a review before its run. Every one was caught by measuring bodies away from the score. Three patterns recur: evolution finds the model’s allowance before the task; fixes were added as options rather than built in; and some instruments could not fail.

The rules a fair experiment must meet

  1. Budget every capacity a body can multiply by adding parts. Above all motor strength, capped for the whole body.
  2. Joints have ranges, so a limb cannot become a free rotor. This is the fix the others lean on.
  3. A body starts at rest. A season with motors off is the “moves by itself” baseline.
  4. The world must pay perception more than its blind substitutes. A perception experiment first shows, on real bodies, that a better nose pays at least about as much as more speed.
  5. Selection must be able to see the gain being tested. Every ecology experiment states its regime: how far income sits above the cost of living, and who breeds.
  6. Compare bodies at stated settings of the genetic operators, which wear down an evolving body’s wiring about five times faster per child.
  7. Name behaviour by instrument, not by yield. “Forages” or “steers” needs a test with a false smell, run beside bodies built to fail it: a blind spinner, a body that only speeds up near food, and a body with its motors off.
  8. Report the body’s levers with every comparison of the two kinds of body. A difference between them that goes with a lever difference is put down to the lever until shown otherwise.
  9. Fairness fixes are switches, but a new comparison will not start without them.
  10. Every control is shown able to fail, and terrain is a main factor, not a robustness check.
  11. Power comes from a bounded model at the real sample size, and no single seed carries a claim.
  12. Corrections propagate. An errata index covers superseded figures, and progress reports correct forward.

Most of the remedies are not new. Sims scaled motor strength and settled bodies (1994); Taylor and Massey added stability checks (2001); Krčah tested bodies for validity before simulating them (2008); Cheney and colleagues charged for actuated material (2013); and Auerbach and Bongard showed that a capacity that costs nothing gets bought whether or not it helps (2014). What the programme adds is the habit of measuring the body apart from the score, before the claim.

Where they stand. The first fixes landed as switches, each off by default and each through its own design, review and ruling: the motor budget, joint ranges, settling, a bound on resting throttle, a smell channel that reads change, and rules for eating. The whole set is now one setting, and a new comparison of the two kinds of body refuses to start without it unless told explicitly.

From one world to a map of worlds

The read. Evolution works in this simulator: both kinds of body respond to selection. What has decided each recent result is the world:

  • Terrain. Cluttered ground taxes wheels. In the default world, with random clutter, the evolving bodies earn more than the fixed body late in a run. In paired forks of the same histories onto flat ground, that lead reverses in 21 of 29.
  • The price of work. How much motor work costs decides whether covering ground pays, and where the lead falls.
  • Food and smell. How food is laid out, and how smell reads, decide whether a nose is worth having. In today’s worlds, moving faster pays more than smelling better.

So any single world quietly picks a winner. Tuning one world until the evolving bodies do something interesting would repeat the first answer’s weight-class mistake in a subtler form.

The plan (the study The world sweep), agreed on 27 September, and where each step stands:

  1. Close the known loopholes, as switchable fixes. Done. A motor budget for the whole body; ranges on every joint; bodies at rest before the clock starts; a limit on the resting throttle; rules for eating; and a better smell channel. Each went through the usual design, review and ruling.

    The motor-budget rerun. The benchmark’s evolving-body side was rerun as registered, with the whole-body motor budget on. It passed independent review with caveats. Under the budget:

    • The evolving bodies still respond to selection, on all 12 seeds: +0.071 net-yield units of up–down divergence per generation [0.060, 0.082], 73% [57%, 95%] of the unbudgeted response.
    • The budget lowers that response, by 27% [5%, 43%]. Almost all of the drop is in the down line, whose wasted work fell from 1.58 to 0.73.
    • “The evolving bodies respond more” is not decided. The margin over the fixed body is +0.13 [−0.19, +0.45]. In food alone, the two tie.
    • It closes one allowance, not all of them. These runs had no joint ranges, and 92% of the down line’s remaining work is still done by limbs touching nothing. The up line still eats by covering ground.
  2. Check the new smell channel on real bodies. Passed, at its registered setting. On evolved fixed bodies fitted with a working compass, the new channel makes the compass pay, through the food itself rather than through motion alone. That shows a working nose pays. Whether a nose pays on the way up from none is a separate question, and not yet tested.
  3. Map how the answer depends on the world. Under way. The first stage is complete, and is the next section.

The deliverable is a map of where each body plan does better. That answers the 2005 question as “it depends on the world, and here is how”, rather than as one verdict hinging on one setting. The proposal’s first reason, strategies that exploit each body’s own quirks, predicts exactly this dependence. The bypass test and the rematch become studies run at chosen points on that map, not in one hand-picked world. Every test is registered in advance, and written so that “the fixed body is as good or better” is a clean result.

New since report 1 · provisional

The world sweep: the first stage

What it asks. Across a grid of worlds, with the loopholes closed, which kind of body earns more, and where? Each point on the grid is one world, set by four dials:

  • Clutter: from flat ground to twice the density of the committed random terrain, in five steps.
  • The price of work: five prices from 0.01 to 0.08 per kilojoule of motor work. The committed price is 0.03.
  • Food layout: uniform (the committed world), patchy, and sparse patches, which regrow slowly in place and are hard to find without smell.
  • Smell: the legacy reading, which reports how strong a smell is, or the new channel, which reports how it is changing.

That makes 150 possible worlds. A cheap census ran every one of them briefly: 3 seeds for 60 seasons. Stage 1 then ran a coarse grid of 36: three clutter levels (flat, normal, double) × three prices (0.01, 0.03, 0.08) × the three layouts with the new channel, plus the same prices and layouts at normal clutter with legacy smell. Each point ran on 8 seeds, for 300 seasons.

Three arms per point.

S, side by side
Each kind of body lives in its own ecology, 60 slots each, as in every committed run. This is the main arm, and it runs everywhere. It measures net income: food eaten minus the price of the motor work spent, per robot per season, in the last 60 seasons.
M, merged
At season 60, a copy of the run is merged into one world with 120 shared slots, so the two kinds of body meet. It ran at the 9 points where the census suggested it could be read.
N, the null
The same merge, but with one kind of body replaced by a relabelled copy of the other. It shows how far the shares drift when there is no real difference. It ran at 4 points.

Seeds. The same 8 seeds are used at every point, so every world starts from the same founders. A founder set is kept only if it can establish in one reference world (flat ground, the committed price, uniform food, legacy smell): at least 30 of 60 robots alive at season 59, each kind on its own. So every result is about founders that can get started there, which is why each comes with the label below.

Registered, then read blind. The design went through several rounds of independent review before anything ran. The readout plan and its script were written and committed before any Stage-1 output was opened, and the integrity check was committed before the readout ran. One slip is on the record: before the readout, the project’s coordinator saw one aggregate count from Stage 1. It was disclosed the same day. The plan had already been committed, and every later ruling by the coordinator is labelled as made after that exposure.

How far this can be read

Independent review accepted the readout with corrections. The coordinator’s ruling adopted them as annotations that govern how the report is read, and changes no call. Three matter here:

  1. The evolving bodies’ side is thin. It is exactly 2 calls, both at points where the evolving bodies do not persist: in one they failed to establish on most seeds, and in the other the fixed body survives better. They were tested on 2 and 3 seeds. One of them passes the multiple-testing threshold by 0.0013 (p 0.0335 against a threshold of 0.0348).
  2. Where both kinds establish, only the fixed body wins any call. At the 15 points where both establish, there is no call for the evolving bodies. Read on those points alone, a reading that was not registered and decides nothing, the result is DEPENDS ONLY THROUGH HABITABILITY, in the fixed body’s favour: the evolving bodies earn more only where they barely live.
  3. A warning flag on every call carries no information. All 8 calls are flagged as resting on a marginal economy. That flag averages income over complete lives only, and in the last 60 seasons of a run the complete lives are mostly the ones that ended early. So at Stage 1 the flag is an artefact of where the run stops, not a fact about the economy.
  • The merged-world comparison could not be read. The null arm failed its own check, so the share layer is void for this stage. That changes no call, because every point with a null arm was already settled by survival.
  • Perception was not measured at Stage 1.
  • Stage 2 can change all of this. The final multiple-testing correction runs once over every point after Stage 2, and the calls are read from that final map.

A bug in the physics engine

The crashed run was traced, after the readout, to a bug in MuJoCo 3.14.0, the physics engine, not in our code.

  • What happens. To find how two overlapping shapes touch, the engine grows a many-sided solid step by step, and keeps a list of the edges where each new step joins the old surface. The list has room for 24 edges, and nothing checks that a 25th will fit. Two parts of one robot, a box and a cylinder on the same parent, had sunk about 0.1 m into each other, comparable to their own size. The next step needed 25 edges. The extra one was written past the end of the list, corrupting memory, and the program crashed.
  • It is deterministic. From the saved state, a single physics step crashes every time. It has been reproduced on four machines. The state was ordinary: nothing had blown up, and no number was infinite.
  • It is an upstream regression. Until version 3.7.0 the list grew with the step limit; from 3.8.0 it is fixed at 24. Others have reported it (google-deepmind/mujoco#3646), and a fix is proposed but not yet merged (#3650).

What it means for the results. The crash was loud. The worry is a quiet one: the same overflow can corrupt a contact without crashing, if the stray write lands somewhere harmless. One overflow has been seen, and it crashed. Scans of about 860 run-seasons found no other, but they did find near misses of 23 edges in ordinary runs. So silent corruption elsewhere in Stage 1 is unlikely but not yet ruled out. A scan of the merged and null runs is approved, at about 100 core-hours; whether to scan the side-by-side runs too, at 600–800 more, is decided on its result.

What changes going forward. The review estimates a 55–75% chance of at least one more overflow in the runs still to come. So every continuation will run on a copy of MuJoCo 3.14.0 built from source with one change: it logs every overflow. Its physics is identical to the stock engine, overflow included, so new runs stay comparable with Stage 1, but no overflow can now pass unseen. Before anything launches on it, the build must be reproducible from a committed recipe and shown identical to the stock engine.

Prior art

These references come from a review of twenty years of related work. An independent citation check found it accurate, with fixes: all 106 DOIs resolve and match their records, there are no phantom citations, and every quotation it could reach is word for word. It corrected one error that mattered here. Sims capped strength per motor, not per joint, so the whole-body cap is our own addition, and only the scaling with cross-section is his. It also asked for the claims of novelty to be hedged to “not found in a directed search”. The fixes are applied below.
