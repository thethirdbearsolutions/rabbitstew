RABBITSTEW · PROGRESS REPORT 3 · 11 OCTOBER 2026 · AS OF 9 OCTOBER, 01:00 UTC

Half of Stage 2 is in, and nothing is read yet

The world sweep asks which kind of robot earns more, and in which worlds. The first half of its second stage has finished. Its integrity check passes, and a rule fixed in advance has chosen 10 points to get more seeds. Under the plan, no call from this half is printed, to anyone, until the final readout. One run unit was set aside because of a gap in our own tooling, not a crash. Elsewhere, the bypass test’s chosen world failed the checks it had to pass before any test could run there, so that study has closed. The study of how mutation proposes a working food compass goes on.

In brief

  • The world sweep, Stage 2a: complete. 12 new worlds, 8 seeds each. Every job finished or was set aside under a ruling. No overflow of the physics-engine bug was logged, and nothing crashed.
  • The interim check: integrity passes. It prints no call, by design. It names the 10 points that go on to 8 more seeds, with merged runs at 2 of them, at an upper bound of 180.6 / 338.1 core-hours, inside the plan’s 221.0 / 413.9.
  • The bypass test: closed. Its world point failed two of the checks it had to pass before the test could run there. An independent review confirmed that the failures are real. This is not an answer to the bypass question. The test simply did not run.
  • The study of mutation’s operators: continuing. Its next runs are written, but not yet proposed for merging.
  • Pending: a decision on scanning the side-by-side runs for silent corruption; a fix to one comparison script before the next batch; the next batch itself, then the final readout; the operator study’s next runs.
New since report 2

The world sweep: the first half of Stage 2

Report 2 described the sweep (the study The world sweep) and its provisional first stage. Each world is set by four dials: clutter, the price of motor work, the food layout and the smell channel. Stage 1 ran a coarse grid of 36 worlds. Stage 2 fills in between them.

The plan. Report 2 said the Stage-2 plan was written and would go to independent review. It has now been reviewed, ruled on and merged. It runs in two halves:

  • 2a adds 12 new worlds, picked from Stage 1’s results by a rule registered before Stage 1 ran. Each runs on the same 8 seeds as Stage 1, for 300 seasons, side by side. Merged runs go at 3 of them, and null runs at 1.
  • 2b gives 8 more seeds to points that are still undecided, to 16 in all. They come from Stage 1’s list (the extension below) and from 2a’s. The Stage-1 part has already run (below); what remains is 2a’s points.
  • Then the final map. The correction for testing many points at once runs over every point, and the registered verdicts are read.

Perception is not measured in Stage 2 either. A calibration found the perception layer unreadable at the strength of planted compass its probes would use, so the probes do not run. Every perception call reads “not measured”, and the registered test of the smell channel enters the final correction as not measured, for good.

No stopping early. On 3 October, before any 2a data existed, the project committed to giving the extra seeds to whatever 2a points the rule names, up to 11 of them, within a fixed budget. So the size of the next batch cannot depend on what 2a shows. That is the guard against choosing to stop, or to go on, after a peek.

One run unit set aside: a gap in a comparison script

Before each world’s first seed carries on to its full 300 seasons, Stage 2 re-runs its first 60 seasons on the logging build of the physics engine. It then checks, byte for byte, that the re-run matches the census run of that world. One such check, in one world (sparse patches, normal clutter, a price of 0.018, legacy smell), on the first seed, refused to proceed.

  • Why it refused. The re-run had stopped at season 38, not 60. The comparison script has no branch for a run that ends early. A later step in the same chain does have one: if every robot has died before the merge at season 60, it records an extinction, which the design registers as a normal outcome. But that step runs after the comparison.
  • What it probably was, and what it was not. In the code, the only way for this phase to finish before season 60 is the ecology stopping because every population is empty. So it was probably a registered early extinction. That is an inference from the refusal message and the code. No run data was read, and the unit is not judged.
  • The ruling. The whole unit is excluded from Stage 2: its side-by-side, merged and null runs, 6 jobs in all. Exclusions work one run folder at a time, and these runs share or read the same folder, so it could not be done in part. The rest of the job list, 15 jobs, ran. The one completed phase is kept but not read. In the readout, the unit’s three runs print as “excluded”, with no crash recorded. Here that word means this ruling, not a fault in the run.
  • Disclosed as a deviation. The ruling was made under a standing delegation from the project lead, and the project lead approved it directly. It is labelled as made after the coordinator’s exposure disclosed in report 2. (A first ruling that day left the lane’s other jobs unrun; it was withdrawn in favour of this one.)
  • The fix comes first. Before 2b runs, the comparison script must accept a run that stopped early only when every population in its saved state is empty, the same test the later step uses. The fix goes through the usual review before any 2b data.

2a finished on 7 October. That last job list, with the excluded unit left out, ran 15 of 15 jobs, logged no overflow and was refused nowhere.

Skipped runs, stated plainly

A merged or null run goes ahead on a seed only if that seed is valid at the merge, meaning both kinds of robot are still alive at season 59. Otherwise it is skipped. The interim does not print the skips as a separate count, but they follow from its numbers: each arm’s “done” minus its clean, overflowed, unlogged and crashed runs. That gives 8 skipped merged runs and 5 skipped null runs (23 done, 15 clean; 7 done, 2 clean). The independent review asked for this to be disclosed, and we state it here so no reader has to work it out. These are survival counts: a skipped run means one kind, or both, had died out on that seed before the merge. They say nothing about which kind, and nothing about income.

It chose 10 points, with merged runs at 2 of them. Their upper bound is 180.6 core-hours at the plan’s low cost per season and 338.1 at its high cost. How the interim was produced

  • The first run produced nothing. A 30-minute background time limit in the session’s harness stopped it. It wrote no interim. The integrity file it had written was overwritten by the re-run.
  • The re-run ran in a separate, designated readout session, authorised directly by the project lead, and finished cleanly (exit code 0).
  • One line of warning output, harmless. An earlier restore of one saved run had been interrupted. A safety marker, built in for this case, made the readout redo that restore before it assembled anything.
  • Independent review: approved, with minor notes. The counts reconcile, the bound recomputes, and no call is printed. Its one note for disclosure is the skipped counts above.

Independent review rebuilt it from scratch and got the same fingerprint. The bypass test: its world failed the gate

The 2005 proposal’s third reason was Conrad’s extradimensional bypass. When a body can change, it may find a way around a fitness valley that blocks a brain evolving in a fixed body. The food compass is such a valley on the fixed body (report 2). The bypass test (registered as Do holistic bodies cross the compass valley more readily than the Pioneer?) was to compare how often each kind of robot evolves and keeps a working compass under selection.

Where it ran. The test was cleared to proceed at one world point only, called W1. W1 was fixed in its registration: sparse food patches, the new smell channel, the committed price of work and normal clutter. It was kept, rather than replaced by a point picked from Stage 1’s map, because a point picked after seeing Stage-1 results would have been chosen with knowledge of the outcome.

The gate. Before any test runs, a world point must pass a set of checks. These ask whether the world pays for perception, and whether the steering test can both detect a compass and stay silent on robots that only look like they steer. A point that fails is reported as “world point failed the gate”, and the test is not run there.

  • Reviewed. An independent review of the gate’s readout confirmed that the failing rows are genuine.
  • Partly expected. Before any W1 data, a pre-registration amendment warned that if the evolving bodies’ planted compasses rarely eat in W1, that check might fail whatever else happened. It noted that this would be the gate measuring what it should.
  • What it means, and what it does not.   • Closed at W1. On 5 October the project decided to close the study at W1 rather than register another world point. About 500–680 core-hours had been accepted for it. The gate and its burn-ins, about 88 of those, were spent. The remainder, the arms’ allowance of about 400–590 core-hours, is now held for a follow-on to the study of mutation’s operators, below.

Changing how mutation proposes a compass

The study Operator changes that let mutation propose a paying compass works on the valley from the other side. Report 2 said mutation proposes the right shape of compass in 84 of 200,000 fixed-body lineages. This study found that none of those arrivals reads as a working compass from its connections alone. At the default step the connection weights never get large enough. With a larger step they can, but the circuit’s resting offsets then swamp them. So it asks whether changes to the genetic operators, the rules by which mutation edits a brain, let mutation propose a compass that pays.

  • Registered after independent design review, on 3–4 October. Two candidate changes on the fixed body are tested formally: a larger step for connection weights, with the step for resting offsets unchanged; and the same with an occasional reset of the offsets. Four more are settled by arithmetic bounds and run only as checks. On the evolving bodies, a census counts two routes to a compass: one through the global brain, and one through duplicated sensor-bearing parts.
  • Its code, with every new switch off by default, passed review and was merged. When it merged, no test condition had yet run.
  • Decision, 5 October: continue.
  • Pending. The job lists for its next runs are written and pushed for review, but not yet proposed for merging. Opening that proposal waits on an action by the operator of its design session.

Three rulings, in plain language

Rulings are how the project settles a case its registered rules did not foresee. Each is written down before it is applied, and says what it knew when it was made.

The holding pilot (bypass test)

The gate included a small trial: plant 8 working compasses in each kind of robot, then check that selection keeps them. As the gate’s code first read the registered rule, the trial drew its 8 plants from one run unit’s robots only. For the evolving bodies, that guaranteed too few, and it refused. The ruling came after the refusal, knowing only that it had happened, not the counts or which kind refused. It widened the pool to plants from every unit that had already been measured, with nothing new run or re-tuned. If either kind still had fewer than 8 plants that paid, the trial would be recorded as “could not be built”. Any result would then carry a stated caveat about holding. That outcome never favours the evolving bodies, and changes no pass or fail.

It could not be built: 105 paying plants for the fixed body, 7 for the evolving bodies.

No rescue after a failed gate (bypass test)
Made after W1 failed. The rules that failed at W1 are not changed after the fact: how the first paying strength is found, and how the evolving bodies’ compass is laid out and counted. Any later analysis that varies them is labelled exploratory and informed by the outcome, and cannot restore the point. A new world point would need its own registered entry and its own gate before any run, and choosing one is a decision for the project, not the analysts.
A fairer sham check (operator study)
Made before any registered condition ran. One control counts compass-shaped circuits built on a sensor that reads other robots, not food. That count must match the food-sensor count, within chance. This shows that a change to mutation is blind to what a sensor senses, and does not favour food sensors. Two conditions wire the food sensors on purpose: the positive control, and one candidate change. There, the food count is high by design, and the comparison would fail by construction. So for those two, the dummy count is compared with the baseline operator’s food count instead. A failure still voids the stage.
